# Abstract

Context: Receptor-preparation choices -- how alternate side-chain conformations, crystallographic waters, and metal ions are handled before docking -- are frequently made silently rather than declared and tested. This work presents DockPrep Audit, a rule-based engine that flags these structural risk factors from a raw PDB file before any preparation step, and tests whether an audit-detected risk factor predicts when a preparation-policy choice changes the redocking result. A 60-case, four-stratum benchmark (15 cases per stratum: alternate location, metal/cofactor, water policy, low-risk control) was frozen with full provenance -- checksums, ten documented case substitutions, two documented chain overrides -- before any policy was applied. Two water-handling policies (conservative, retaining site-local bridging water; simplified, removing all water) were compared across three seeds per case, and, separately, two altLoc-resolution policies (highest occupancy, the project default; lowest occupancy, the symmetric opposite) and two metal policies (retain, the project default; remove) were each compared on the 15 cases with the corresponding site-local audit-flagged feature. RMSD was corrected for molecular symmetry throughout (minimum over ligand heavy-atom automorphisms, not a rigid-body realignment), which changed the exact-atom-index value originally computed for 480 of 546 runs and, in two comparisons, changed which pattern the paired significance test detects; both values are reported where they differ materially. The water-policy stratum showed the only stratum-level effect of the four strata under the water manipulation; restricted to the 11 of 15 cases confirmed by the audit engine to contain an Eq.-1-qualifying bridging water and to have usable symmetry data, best-of-nine-poses success fraction was 0.697 vs. 0.576 and mean RMSD 2.719 Å vs. 2.952 Å, concentrated in 5 of those 11 cases, though it did not reach significance in a paired Wilcoxon test at this cohort size (p=0.21) and was invisible under a binary top-ranked-pose threshold. This result was then checked against an independently assembled, 76-case external validation cohort, screened for the same bridging-water finding but built without any manual curation toward a particular ligand chemistry: its *direction* did not replicate (best-of-nine success 0.789 vs. 0.803, paired Wilcoxon p=0.78, 10 of 76 cases favoring water retention against 11 favoring its removal) -- reported here as a genuine non-replication, not reframed as a smaller or more nuanced version of the original finding. A formal predictive evaluation of the audit finding as a binary classifier of policy sensitivity (all 60 main-cohort cases, n=59) found weak precision (PPV 0.257) but a highly significant difference in effect *magnitude* between flagged and unflagged cases (mean |ΔRMSD| 0.329 Å vs. 0.023 Å, Mann-Whitney p<0.0001) -- and this magnitude relationship, unlike the water-policy direction, was corroborated by the validation cohort (mean/median |ΔRMSD| 0.372/0.097 Å, matching the main cohort's flagged-case values, not its unflagged ones). The altLoc-policy comparison (n=15) showed a comparable-magnitude effect concentrated in 4 of 15 cases, split almost evenly in direction (3 favoring the default policy, 1 favoring the opposite by a comparable magnitude) rather than one-directional as water was; unlike the exact-index value, this comparison does not approach significance (p=0.21) once a symmetry-labeling artifact in one case's ligand is corrected. The metal-policy comparison (n=15) showed the least concentrated effect of the three (2 of 15 cases) but the single largest per-case effect of the entire study (best-of-nine RMSD 4.177 Å vs. 1.421 Å for one zinc metalloprotease case, 1DTH), in the direction of removing the metal outperforming the project's retain-by-default policy -- and, after symmetry correction removed one case whose apparent effect ran the other way, this is the only one of the three comparisons to reach conventional statistical significance within its own cohort (p=0.0084), though it was not itself externally validated and is confounded with a simultaneous change in coordinating-histidine protonation state that prevents isolating the metal atom's own contribution.

Methods: All 60 sources were retrieved from RCSB with recorded checksums and audited before any preparation policy was chosen. Receptors and ligands were prepared with Meeko 0.7.1 (altLoc by highest occupancy by default, or lowest occupancy for the dedicated altLoc-policy comparison; metals retained by default with geometrically assigned coordinating-histidine tautomers, or removed with those tautomers reverted to Meeko's default for the dedicated metal-policy comparison; ligands protonated at pH 7.4) and redocked with AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 at exhaustiveness 32. Ligand-reference correspondence used exact coordinate matching (0.01 Å) rather than Meeko's own atom-index output, with RMSD subsequently minimized over ligand heavy-atom automorphisms to correct for molecular symmetry. Cohort quality was checked against wwPDB validation metrics (RSCC/RSR ≥0.8 for 49/60 cases) and biological-assembly agreement with `REMARK 350` (59/60 cases). Twenty of 60 cases (33%) share a biological target with the author's prior 102-target DUD-E receptor-preparation audit; this overlap is disclosed quantitatively, not hidden. An independent, 76-case external validation cohort was assembled from RCSB by the same site-local bridging-water finding, excluding every identifier already used in this or the two prior studies, with all analysis definitions fixed before it was screened.

Keywords: molecular docking; receptor preparation; reproducibility; AutoDock Vina; structural audit; crystallographic water; benchmark

# 1 Introduction

Molecular docking is frequently used to generate structural hypotheses in computer-aided molecular research, and its practical interpretation depends on the receptor structure that is actually presented to the docking engine [1,2]. Before any pose is scored, a receptor must first be *prepared*: alternate side-chain conformations must be resolved to a single representative, crystallographic waters must be kept or discarded, and metal ions or non-standard residues must be assigned a chemically valid representation. These decisions are frequently made silently -- absorbed into a preparation script's defaults -- rather than declared and justified, which makes it difficult for a reader to know whether a reported docking result depends on the receptor's raw structural features or is robust to how those features were handled.

A structure can be audited for the presence of these risk factors before any preparation choice is made. Alternate locations (altLoc), non-water-stripped crystallographic waters, and bound or coordinating metal ions are all directly detectable from the deposited coordinates themselves, independent of any downstream docking protocol. This raises a narrower, testable question than "is this docking result correct": whether the *presence* of an audit-detectable structural risk factor predicts *when* a change in preparation policy will change the docking outcome. If it does, an audit performed before preparation could serve as a cheap triage signal -- flagging which receptors are worth testing under more than one preparation policy, rather than requiring every study to do so exhaustively.

This work differs from two prior studies by the author's group that use an overlapping set of structures or techniques. The first is a 102-target audit of receptor-preparation compatibility across the DUD-E benchmark (`dude_receptor_prep_audit`, submitted to this same journal) [3], which asked whether a declared direct preparation route could process a fixed set of source structures at all, and made no claim about docking-outcome sensitivity to preparation choices. The second is a prior manuscript on alternate-conformation handling and pose recovery on an earlier, related cohort [4]. The present study asks a different question from both: not whether preparation succeeds, and not how to recover a single reference pose, but whether a structural feature that can be flagged *before* preparation predicts *when* choosing between two legitimate preparation policies changes the redocking result. Overlap between this cohort and the identifiers used in both prior studies was checked systematically rather than assumed absent: a combined registry of 132 previously used PDB identifiers from both projects was cross-referenced against every candidate before it was frozen into the present cohort, and one candidate already present in that registry was withdrawn during selection. Overlap at the level of PDB identifier was avoidable and was avoided; overlap at the level of biological target was not fully avoidable without abandoning structurally rich, well-characterized targets that the field already studies most, and is disclosed quantitatively rather than hidden (Section 5).

The contribution of this work is fourfold: (1) an open, rule-based audit engine (DockPrep Audit v0.2.0) that flags alternate-location, crystallographic-water, and metal/non-standard-residue risk factors directly from a raw PDB file, before any preparation step, plus site-local variants of these findings evaluated relative to a declared ligand instance; (2) a 60-case, four-stratum (15 cases per stratum) redocking benchmark with fully frozen provenance -- source checksums, documented substitutions, and documented receptor-chain overrides where a purely geometric rule disagreed with the deposited biological assembly; (3) mixed, cohort-dependent evidence, from three independent preparation-policy manipulations (water retention, altLoc resolution, and metal retention) each re-evaluated with a symmetry-corrected redocking-accuracy metric, that an audit-flagged risk category can predict preparation-policy sensitivity within a given cohort -- one-directional and descriptive for water, evenly split in direction and not statistically significant for altLoc, and the least concentrated of the three but the only one to reach conventional significance within its own cohort for metal; and (4) a discovery/validation check on the water-policy result specifically, using an independently assembled 76-case cohort with every analysis definition fixed in advance, which found that the effect's *direction* did not replicate but its *magnitude* relationship did (a formal predictive evaluation, Section 3.10) -- reported as a real limit on how far the within-cohort findings generalize, not smoothed into either a softer or a fully negative version of the original claim.

# 2 Materials and methods

## 2.1 The DockPrep Audit engine

DockPrep Audit (v0.2.0) is a rule-based engine that inspects a raw PDB file and reports structural risk factors without altering the coordinates or choosing a preparation policy on the user's behalf. Three whole-structure finding classes are detected directly from the deposited ATOM/HETATM records, independent of any declared ligand (Table 1). From v0.2.0, when a declared ligand instance is supplied, three additional site-local findings are evaluated relative to that ligand, closing a gap between what earlier versions of the engine reported and what this study's cohort stratification actually used: `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` applies Eq. 1 (Section 2.3) directly as an engine finding, rather than leaving it as an analysis-only criterion computed outside the audited tool. Each finding is reported with its residue-level locations and an explanatory message stating that the corresponding preparation decision is protocol-dependent and must be documented, not that any specific resolution is correct. The engine's output is a versioned JSON report per structure; no coordinate file is modified at this stage.

**Table 1** DockPrep Audit finding classes

| Finding code | Severity | Detection rule | Preparation decision it flags |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ALTLOC_PRESENT` | High | Any atom record with a non-blank alternate-location identifier | Which conformer to retain (Section 2.3) |
| `WATERS_PRESENT` | Medium | Any HETATM record with residue name HOH | Whether a given water is retained (whole-structure rule; distinct from the site-local bridging-water criterion of Eq. 1) |
| `METAL_PRESENT` | Medium | Any HETATM record matching a recognized metal-ion residue name | Coordination/charge handling and, where applicable, coordinating-histidine tautomer |
| `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` | Medium | Water oxygen within 3.0 Å of both a declared ligand atom and a receptor atom (Eq. 1) | Whether the conservative water policy retains this specific water (Section 2.3) |
| `SITE_ALTLOC_PRESENT` | High | Alternate-location atom within 6 Å of the declared ligand | Whether the flagged conformer sits in the binding site itself, not merely elsewhere in the structure |
| `SITE_METAL_PRESENT` | Medium | Metal ion within 6 Å of the declared ligand | Whether the flagged metal is binding-site-proximal rather than a remote crystallographic ion |

## 2.2 Cohort construction and freezing

A pilot cohort of 12 structures (3 per risk stratum: alternate location, metal/cofactor, water policy, and a low-risk control stratum with no high-severity finding) was assembled first and frozen -- source URL, byte count, and SHA-256 checksum recorded for every structure -- before any preparation policy was applied or any docking result was observed. The cohort was subsequently expanded to 60 structures (15 per stratum, Table S1) by repeating the same search-and-audit process: candidates were retrieved from RCSB by full-text and keyword search, audited with the same engine, and filtered by a site-local spatial-relevance rule requiring the flagged feature (altLoc, metal, or bridging water) to lie within 6 Å of the declared ligand instance -- a distance chosen to capture the ligand's first and near-second solvation/contact shell without admitting features on a remote, functionally unrelated part of a large receptor, and applied identically across strata so no stratum's inclusion criterion is stricter than another's. A cap of two cases per biological target (by UniProt accession) was applied during the expansion after an initial search returned six of thirteen alternate-location candidates as the same protein, to avoid a single target dominating a stratum.

## 2.3 Preparation policies

All preparation decisions were fixed and documented before any docking result was observed. Two altLoc-resolution policies were compared, mirroring the water-policy comparison below: the highest-occupancy policy (used for every case not in the dedicated altLoc-policy comparison of Section 3.6, and the project's original default) retains the conformer of highest reported occupancy, ties broken to conformer 'A' as declared in the file; the lowest-occupancy policy retains the conformer of lowest reported occupancy instead, ties broken to the last declared letter -- the symmetric-opposite manipulation, applied only to the 15 cases with a confirmed site-local alternate conformer (`SITE_ALTLOC_PRESENT`, Section 2.1), holding the water policy fixed at conservative for that comparison. Both policies are structural-fidelity decisions (which conformation most likely existed in the crystal), not preparation-philosophy choices, and every choice is logged per residue.

Two water-handling policies were compared. A water molecule *w* was retained under the conservative policy if and only if

$$d(w_O, \text{ligand}) \leq 3.0\ \text{Å} \quad \text{and} \quad d(w_O, \text{receptor}) \leq 3.0\ \text{Å} \qquad (1)$$

where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the Euclidean distance between the water oxygen and the nearest heavy atom of the specified partner; the 3.0 Å cutoff is the conventional upper bound for a hydrogen-bond-range polar contact and was applied identically on both sides of the bridge, so the criterion is symmetric rather than biased toward the ligand or the receptor. A retained water was modeled as a rigid oxygen carrying the TIP3P point charge (−0.834 *e*). The simplified policy removed all water unconditionally. A third, originally planned "open" policy was found to be computationally identical to the simplified policy in this pipeline and was merged with it rather than reported as a spurious third condition.

Eq. 1 is stricter than the screening rule used to assemble the water-policy stratum (Section 2.2): cohort selection required only a water within 4.0 Å of the declared ligand, evaluated against the ligand alone, whereas Eq. 1 additionally requires receptor contact within the tighter 3.0 Å bound. This discrepancy was not by design; it was found after the fact, once Eq. 1 was implemented directly in the audit engine as the `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` finding (Section 2.1) and run against all 60 frozen sources, rather than being left as an analysis-only calculation computed separately from the audited tool. Three of the 15 water-policy-stratum cases (1HRN, 1PPM, 3FNU) satisfied the looser selection screen but contained no water meeting Eq. 1: for these three, the conservative and simplified policies produce an identical receptor input, so any observed RMSD difference between them is Vina's own multi-threaded non-determinism (Limitation 6), not a water-policy effect. This is reported rather than corrected retroactively in the cohort composition, since retroactively reselecting an already-frozen stratum would violate the freeze-before-observing-results protocol used throughout (Section 2.2); instead, Section 3.5 reports the stratum-level water-policy comparison on the 12 cases where Eq. 1 confirms a manipulable bridging water actually exists, alongside the full 15-case figures for transparency, and Table S3 retains RMSD values for all 15.

Receptors and ligands were prepared to PDBQT format with Meeko 0.7.1 [5]. Two metal policies were compared, mirroring the water- and altLoc-policy comparisons above: the retain policy (project default, used everywhere outside the dedicated metal-policy comparison of Section 3.7) keeps metal ions using native residue templates where available (Zn, Mg, Ca, Mn, Fe) and assigns coordinating-histidine tautomer geometrically (distance to the coordinated metal) rather than by Meeko's default, since eight of twelve zinc-coordinating histidines in the pilot cohort required the non-default tautomer; the remove policy drops metal ions from the receptor entirely and, applied only to the 15 cases with a confirmed site-local metal (`SITE_METAL_PRESENT`, Section 2.1), lets any histidine that would otherwise have been geometrically constrained fall back to Meeko's own default tautomer instead, since forcing a tautomer by distance to a metal that is no longer part of the model has no physical justification. This means the metal-policy manipulation is not a pure atom-presence test: removing the metal simultaneously reverts the protonation state of its coordinating histidines, a coupling discussed further in Section 3.7 and Limitation 8. Ligands were protonated at physiological pH (7.4); when multiple protonation variants were proposed, each was attempted in order and the first chemically valid molecule was retained, without re-ranking by docking outcome.

The docking box was a cube centered on the ligand centroid, with side length

$$L = 2.9 \times R_g \qquad (2)$$

where $R_g$ is the ligand's radius of gyration [6], rather than a fixed or manually tuned padding around the ligand bounding box. This reproduces ref. [6] verbatim -- "the dimensions of the search space are 2.9 times larger than the radius of gyration," validated on ligands of 6-100 heavy atoms with a cubic box -- with no coefficient adjustment; that source states no explicit caveat for elongated or very small ligands, but the formula's Rg dependence implicitly assumes a roughly isotropic ligand shape, since Rg is a single scalar and cannot represent an elongated, rod-like molecule's greater extent along its long axis. Whether this assumption holds across the present cohort's actual ligand shapes was checked directly rather than assumed (Section 3.3, Limitation 11).

## 2.4 Redocking validation and metrics

Each case was redocked with AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 [7,8] under the fixed parameters in Table 2. Exhaustiveness was set to 32, four times Vina's own default of 8, so that a sampling failure could be attributed to the receptor/ligand geometry or the preparation policy rather than to an under-searched conformational space; the same elevated setting was used for every case, policy, and seed, so it cannot itself explain the between-policy or between-stratum differences reported in Section 3. This reflects Vina's documented non-determinism under multi-threaded execution even with a fixed seed -- three predeclared seeds were run per case and policy rather than one, for this reason.

**Table 2** AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 run parameters, fixed for every case × policy × seed combination

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Exhaustiveness | 32 |
| Number of modes | 9 |
| CPU | 0 (all available, multi-threaded) |
| Seeds (3 per case × policy) | 42, 123, 2024 |
| Box shape / size | Cube, side $L = 2.9 \times R_g$ (Eq. 2) |
| Box center | Ligand centroid |

Because Meeko's torsion-tree writer does not preserve input atom order, correspondence between the prepared ligand and the deposited reference pose was established by exact coordinate matching (0.01 Å tolerance) rather than by trusting any atom-index hint in the output file. Accuracy was quantified as the heavy-atom root-mean-square deviation between the docked pose and the deposited reference pose after this exact atom-to-atom correspondence was established:

$$\text{RMSD} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_i^{\text{ref}} \rVert^2} \qquad (3)$$

for the $N$ matched heavy atoms; no case in the frozen cohort failed this correspondence step; had one occurred, it would have been treated as a technical-validation failure and substituted under the same protocol as the cases in Table S2.

Eq. 3 as stated is exact-atom-index RMSD: atom $i$ of the docked pose is compared to atom $i$ of the reference, using the identity established by the coordinate matching above. This penalizes a chemically meaningless labeling choice whenever the ligand contains a locally symmetric group (e.g. a carboxylate's two oxygens, a ring's mirror-equivalent substituent positions): swapping two symmetry-equivalent atoms' labels changes Eq. 3's value without changing the physical pose at all. The RMSD reported throughout the main text and figures is therefore symmetry-corrected: for each docked pose, every heavy-atom automorphism of the ligand consistent with its molecular graph and stereochemistry (`rdkit.Chem.rdmolops.GetSubstructMatches(mol, mol, useChirality=True)` against the ligand as built for docking) is enumerated, and the reported RMSD is the minimum of Eq. 3 over that set -- a fixed-frame recalculation, not a rigid-body realignment, since realigning the pose would defeat the purpose of a docking-accuracy metric (a correctly shaped but badly placed pose must still score badly). This changed the reported RMSD for 480 of 546 runs across the three policy comparisons (mean top-1 correction 0.13-0.25 Å depending on comparison) and, in two cases, changed which stratum-level pattern the paired test detects (Sections 3.5-3.7); the original exact-index values are retained in Table S3 for traceability. One case (1OHR) could not be evaluated this way and keeps its exact-index value throughout: the chosen protonation-variant SMILES for its ligand (component 1UN) resolves to 40 heavy atoms against 44 in the deposited coordinates, an RCSB chemical-component data discrepancy unrelated to receptor preparation, not a defect in this pipeline; excluding it changes stratum sample sizes by at most one case, noted where it applies. Redocking outcome was then classified in three categories following standard redocking-benchmark practice, distinguishing sampling failure from scoring failure [9]:

$$
\text{outcome} =
\begin{cases}
\text{success} & \text{RMSD}_{\text{top-1}} \leq 2.0\ \text{Å} \\
\text{scoring\_fail} & \text{RMSD}_{\text{top-1}} > 2.0\ \text{Å} \text{ and } \min_i \text{RMSD}_i \leq 2.0\ \text{Å} \\
\text{sampling\_fail} & \min_i \text{RMSD}_i > 2.0\ \text{Å}
\end{cases}
\qquad (4)
$$

where $\text{RMSD}_{\text{top-1}}$ is the RMSD of Vina's top-ranked pose and $\min_i \text{RMSD}_i$ is the best RMSD among all nine generated poses. Both $\text{RMSD}_{\text{top-1}}$ and $\min_i \text{RMSD}_i$ are reported for every case, because an earlier stage of this study found that reducing the water-policy comparison to the binary top-ranked-pose outcome concealed a real, reproducible effect that only appeared when considering the full pose set.

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Cohort characteristics

Across the frozen 60-case cohort, the crystallographic-water finding was present in 59/60 structures, alternate locations in 22/60, and metal ions in 18/60 (Fig. 1). Twenty-two cases showed both alternate locations and waters, seventeen showed both waters and a metal, and four showed both alternate locations and a metal. The near-ubiquity of the water finding is expected: it is the whole-structure, any-water rule from the audit engine, distinct from the site-local bridging-water criterion (Section 2.3) used to define the water-policy stratum, and the two should not be conflated when reading Fig. 1.

## 3.2 Cohort quality control

[FIGURE 2: fig_qc_rscc_by_year.png]

Ligand identity and pose reliability were checked quantitatively against wwPDB validation metrics [10] for every case, rather than assumed from the audit engine's structural findings alone. Real-space correlation coefficient (RSCC) and real-space R-factor (RSR) were available for 49 of 60 cases; of these, all 49 exceeded the field's typical reliability threshold of RSCC ≥0.8 (range 0.817-0.978), and none showed a chemical-identity mismatch between the declared ligand component, its deposited name, and the structure's own title. The remaining 11 cases lack RSCC/RSR because no structure-factor file is available at RCSB for that deposition -- a gap that correlates with, but is not a clean function of, deposition era (Fig. 2): five predate 1998 (when structure-factor deposition was not yet routine), but six further cases deposited between 1999 and 2004 were confirmed, by direct query, to also lack a structure-factor file, while structures deposited as early as 1987 do have one available. Biological-assembly chain assignment agreed between the purely geometric 6 Å rule and the deposited `REMARK 350` record for 59 of 60 cases; the one disagreement (1SN5, described in Section 2.2) was resolved by an explicit, documented override rather than by silently accepting either source.

## 3.3 Pipeline robustness

All 360 redocking runs (60 cases × 2 policies × 3 seeds, at exhaustiveness 32) completed without a preparation error on the frozen cohort. Reaching that point required a documented substitution pass: on a first technical pass over the 48 expansion cases, 12 failed -- three for a transient network condition, resolved on retry, and nine for the genuine tool/chemistry limitations described in Section 2.2 and listed in Table S2, each resolved by a substitution verified independently against the same eligibility and technical-validation criteria as the rest of the cohort before acceptance. This failure-and-substitution record is reported as evidence that the audit-and-substitution protocol is itself auditable, not as a defect in the cohort.

The box-sizing formula ($L=2.9\times R_g$, Eq. 2) was checked against every case's actual deposited ligand geometry rather than assumed adequate from the source paper's own validation. Because $R_g$ is a single scalar, the formula implicitly assumes an isotropic ligand shape; checking the ligand's axis-aligned bounding-box extent against the resulting cubic box directly tests whether that assumption holds here. It does not, for a substantial minority: in 13 of 60 cases (22%), the ligand's longest extent exceeds the box side length, by up to 2.0 Å (1QRP). This is concentrated among the cohort's most elongated ligands (9 of the 10 highest elongation ratios, defined as longest extent divided by shortest, have a zero or negative margin) rather than among its smallest ones -- every case with fewer than 15 heavy atoms has a comfortable positive margin (0.6-1.6 Å) -- confirming the a priori concern is about ligand shape, not size. The practical consequence is a modest, not catastrophic, accuracy penalty: the 13 undersized-box cases have a lower best-of-9 success fraction than the other 47 (0.692 vs. 0.804) and a higher mean RMSD (2.57 Å vs. 1.69 Å), but they are not the cohort's worst failures -- only 2 of the 13 (1SN5, 1EED) reach the ~9 Å range that characterizes this study's hardest cases, and both have an independently identified, more specific explanation already (Section 3.4, Section 3.6) rather than box size alone. This is reported as a genuine, quantified limitation of the formula as applied here (Limitation 11), not smoothed over because the formula is a cited, published rule.

## 3.4 Per-case redocking outcomes

[FIGURE 3: fig_heatmap_case_policy.png] [FIGURE 4: fig_rmsd_distribution.png]

The per-case best-of-nine-poses RMSD under each policy, grouped by stratum, is shown in Fig. 3; the corresponding distributions are shown in Fig. 4; the complete numeric table underlying both figures is given in Table S3. Most cases are insensitive to the water policy by construction of the comparison itself: the low-risk-control case 1A28, for instance, reaches an identical 0.356 Å RMSD and a 100% top-ranked-pose success rate under both policies, since no water is close enough to the ligand to be affected by either rule. At the other extreme, 6ASH shows a large, policy-dependent gap in the best-of-nine metric (3.303 Å with bridging water retained vs. 4.720 Å without) despite failing the 2.0 Å top-ranked-pose threshold under both policies -- an example of the scoring-vs-sampling distinction motivating the dual RMSD reporting in Section 2.4.

Two water-policy cases, 1EPP and 1PPM, classify as *sampling_fail* consistently across all three seeds under both policies (best-of-9 RMSD 9.257-9.721 Å and 9.178 Å respectively) -- the largest RMSD values in the cohort. Both are aspartic-protease/peptidomimetic-inhibitor complexes substituted into the cohort as replacements for pepstatin-class cases that failed preparation entirely (Table S2, Section 2.2), and both remain difficult dockings for AutoDock Vina regardless of water policy: this is reported rather than smoothed over, since it indicates the substitution protocol replaced a preparation-stage failure with a genuine, reproducible docking-stage difficulty for this ligand class, not a preparation problem in disguise. The two cases still differ from each other in one respect worth noting precisely: 1PPM is fully policy-insensitive (9.178 Å under both policies, identical to three decimals), while 1EPP is not -- it is one of the cases counted among the water-policy stratum's benefiting cases in Table 4 (9.257 Å with bridging water vs. 9.721 Å without), even though neither policy brings it anywhere near the 2.0 Å success threshold. This difference has a concrete cause, not a coincidental one: 1PPM is one of the three cases (Section 2.3) with no water actually meeting the Eq. 1 bridging criterion, so its conservative and simplified inputs are identical and no policy-dependent shift is mechanistically possible; 1EPP does have a confirmed bridging water and shows a real, if modest, RMSD shift as a result. A case can therefore show a real, reproducible policy-dependent RMSD shift while remaining a sampling failure under both conditions -- the stratum-level effect in Table 3/4 is about relative pose quality, not about crossing the success threshold.

Why 1EPP and 1PPM fail so consistently was investigated directly rather than left as an unexplained outlier pair, checking four candidate explanations. Docking-box adequacy is not the general explanation: 1PPM's ligand (12.9 Å maximum extent) fits comfortably inside its 17.5 Å box, and while 1EPP's ligand (16.5 Å extent inside a 17.2 Å box) fits only tightly, a third case with an even larger extent-to-box ratio (1QRP, ligand extent 20.9 Å against an 18.9 Å box -- extent *exceeding* the box) still reaches a 1.8 Å best-of-9 RMSD, so a tight or even nominally undersized box does not by itself predict failure in this cohort. Ligand protonation is not the explanation either: both ligands' SMILES-to-3D protonation succeeded on the first attempted variant with no fallback needed (Section 2.3), unlike the cases in Table S2 that failed at this step entirely. Ligand flexibility is a real, contributing factor, but not a sufficient one on its own: 1EPP (23 rotatable bonds) is the single most flexible ligand in the 60-case cohort, and 1PPM (15) is in the upper third; both are large peptidomimetic aspartic-protease inhibitors, consistent with their substitution history (Table S2). More strikingly, the entire water-policy stratum is far more flexible on average than the other three (mean 14 rotatable bonds per ligand, median 15, vs. 5.1-7.9 for the other strata) -- a real, previously undocumented imbalance, most plausibly because a site-local bridging water is itself a well-characterized structural feature of aspartic-protease active sites in the crystallographic literature, so screening for it (Section 2.2) incidentally enriches this stratum for that ligand class. All three of the stratum's worst-performing cases (1EPP, 1EED, 1PPM; RMSD 8.9-9.3 Å) are among its six most flexible ligands, but three other similarly flexible cases in the same stratum (1QRP, 1WBK, 1WBM; 15-21 rotatable bonds) dock successfully, so flexibility raises risk without being deterministic, and what specifically separates the failing three from the successful three at comparable flexibility is not resolved here. The fourth candidate, a poor-quality multiconformational reference pose, cannot be ruled out for either case: both 1EPP (1994) and 1PPM (1992) are among the 11 cases in this cohort with no available structure-factor file (Section 3.2), so their deposited reference coordinates cannot be independently checked against RSCC/RSR the way 49 of the 60 cases can be -- if either reference itself carries unmodeled ambiguity in a large, flexible ligand, from an era when refining such a pose was harder than it is now, that alone would inflate every redocking RMSD reported for it, independent of receptor preparation or docking search quality. This is reported as an open, partially-resolved question -- flexibility and reference-quality both remain plausible, unfalsified contributors -- rather than a closed one.

The two metrics tell different stories for the water-policy stratum, and the contrast is itself informative. The top-1 success fraction is identical between policies (0.364 vs. 0.364) -- by that metric alone, the water policy would appear to have no effect at all, in every stratum. Only the best-of-9 metric reveals a gap, concentrated in the water-policy stratum and absent elsewhere (Table 3): a +0.121 success-fraction gap and a 0.233 Å mean-RMSD gap, both larger than the exact-index values reported for the same 11 cases before symmetry correction (+0.111 and 0.196 Å respectively at n=12 including 1OHR), because the correction removed a labeling artifact that had been working against, not for, the effect (Section 2.4 methodology; no individual water-policy case's correction exceeded 0.5 Å, unlike the altLoc and metal comparisons below). This is the stratum-level analogue of a pattern already observed at the single-case level during the earlier 12-case pilot phase of this study: a policy effect that changes whether Vina *samples* a near-native pose can be fully masked by a binary top-ranked-pose threshold, because that threshold also depends on the scoring function's ranking of poses it already sampled. Seed-to-seed instability of the top-1 outcome category is low and roughly uniform across strata (1-3 of 15 cases per stratum × policy), so this masking is not primarily a seed-variance artifact.

The water-policy stratum is the only one of the four showing a stratum-level difference between policies, in the direction expected if bridging water is genuinely relevant to the docking pose. At the per-case level, this aggregate is not driven by a single outlier: of the 11 water-policy cases with a genuine bridging water and usable symmetry data, 5 show a clear benefit from retaining it (RMSD improvement >0.3 Å: 1CVZ, 1EPP, 1WBK, 4GID, 6ASH), 1 shows the opposite (2F25), and 5 show no discernible effect despite a real water being present and manipulated (1EED moves from "hurts" to "no effect" under symmetry correction, −0.311 Å to −0.280 Å, no longer crossing the 0.3 Å threshold). By contrast, the other three strata each show only 1-2 of 15 cases with a comparable effect in either direction (alternate location: 1M17, 2I4H benefit, 5E0J opposed; metal/cofactor: 1KJO benefit, 3LXE opposed; low-risk control: 3BEJ benefit, none opposed).

A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the best-of-9 RMSD (conservative vs. simplified) does not reach conventional significance in any stratum, including water policy (n=11, W=18.0, p=0.21, mean paired difference −0.233 Å in favor of the conservative policy); the other three strata give p=0.62, 0.33, and 0.78 respectively. This is closer to significance than the exact-index result reported for the same comparison before symmetry correction (p=0.28-0.38 across the n=12/n=15 variants; Table S3), consistent with the correction removing a case that opposed the trend, but is still reported plainly rather than oversold: this benchmark is underpowered to establish stratum-level significance by a formal paired test, and the water-policy finding should be read as descriptive evidence -- the largest, most one-directional, and most case-consistent effect of the four strata under the water manipulation -- not a statistically confirmed result. A larger, purpose-powered follow-up cohort, screened directly with the `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` finding rather than the looser 4.0 Å rule used here, would be needed to test the finding at conventional significance.

A stricter geometric hypothesis was tested against the original 12-case pilot before the cohort was expanded: that the effect should correlate with whether the retained water forms a hydrogen-bond-range contact (≤3.2 Å) with a ligand heteroatom, rather than merely being present near the ligand. Nine of the twelve pilot cases met that criterion, including two low-risk-control cases that showed no measurable docking effect; only one case showed a large effect despite eight other cases meeting the identical geometric criterion. This hypothesis is reported as not supported: presence of a hydrogen-bond-range contact does not by itself distinguish which cases are sensitive to the water-retention policy, in either the pilot or the expanded cohort.

As a robustness check on the specific 2.0 Å success threshold used throughout this study (Section 2.4), the conservative-minus-simplified success-fraction gap was recomputed across a continuous range of thresholds (0.5-5.0 Å, **Fig. S1**, Online Resource 1) for all four strata. The water-policy gap is positive at 2.0 Å and remains positive across most of this range, which rules out the threshold being an isolated, cherry-picked point of maximal effect. It is not, however, monotonic -- at n=15 cases per stratum, the gap moves in discrete 1/15 steps as individual cases cross each threshold, producing visible noise -- and it is not the largest or most sustained gap among the four strata at every threshold: the alternate-location stratum shows a comparably sized gap through most of the range and a larger one at 3.2-4.7 Å under this *water*-policy manipulation (the only one tested here across all four strata uniformly). This is discussed further as Limitation 7; Section 3.6 reports a separate, dedicated altLoc-policy manipulation on this same stratum, which is not what Fig. S1 shows.

A separate concern is the water representation itself, not the policy comparison: a bare, undirected oxygen point charge (Section 2.3) cannot express hydrogen-bond directionality, so part of the reported effect could in principle be an artifact of that specific, simplified representation rather than of retaining a water molecule as such. As a targeted check, the four water-policy-stratum cases with the largest effect (Table 4: 6ASH, 1WBK, 1CVZ, 4GID) were re-run with each retained water modeled instead as a rigid three-atom TIP3P body (O-H bond length 0.9572 Å, H-O-H angle 104.52°, full TIP3P partial charges: O −0.834 *e*, each H +0.417 *e*), its two hydrogens oriented toward the same ligand and receptor atoms that had qualified it as bridging in the first place (Eq. 1), rather than toward independently chosen or randomized targets. All four cases retained the water-helps direction under this richer representation, and three of the four (6ASH, 1WBK, 1CVZ) showed an equal or larger effect than the bare-oxygen model (+2.581, +0.548, +0.484 Å respectively, vs. +1.417, +0.497, +0.480 Å); the fourth (4GID) showed a smaller effect that falls just under the 0.3 Å non-negligible-effect threshold used throughout this study (+0.251 Å vs. +0.485 Å), though it remains in the same direction. This is a small, targeted check (n=4, not a full-cohort re-run) rather than a formal statistical test, but it weighs against the water representation itself being the source of the effect: a bare, direction-less point charge was, in this specific subset, if anything a conservative model of the phenomenon rather than an inflated one.

Inspecting the full 9-pose profile shows this is a sampling effect, not a scoring effect: the top-ranked pose is nearly identical under both policies across all three seeds (7.78-7.92 Å, always a clear sampling failure), but the best pose among the nine sampled crosses the 2.0 Å threshold only when the metal is removed (1.40-1.74 Å across seeds, vs. 2.19-4.24 Å with the metal retained) -- removing the metal lets Vina's search access a near-native pose it otherwise never samples, though the scoring function still fails to rank it first (outcome shifts from *sampling_fail* to *scoring_fail*, not to *success*).

This result requires the caveat flagged in Section 2.3: the metal-removal manipulation is not a pure atom-presence test, because removing the metal also reverts its coordinating histidines from a geometrically forced tautomer to Meeko's default. For 1DTH specifically, all three coordinating histidines are forced to HID under the retain policy and revert to Meeko's HIE-first default under remove -- so the observed effect could reflect the missing metal atom, the changed histidine protonation states, or their combination, and this study cannot distinguish between those explanations. This coupling is not unique to 1DTH: checking the coordinating-histidine count directly, 1CBX, 5A2S, and 4EXS also have 2-4 histidines whose tautomer reverts under the remove policy, yet show no comparable docking effect (|Δ| ≤0.3 Å, not among the 2 cases in Table 8) -- so the tautomer reversion alone does not generically produce a large effect, which argues against it being a trivial confound that would appear in every case, but does not rule it out as part of the specific mechanism at 1DTH's active site. Disentangling the two would require a dedicated third policy (metal removed, tautomer still geometrically forced) not run here; that this comparison is now the study's one formally significant result (above) makes resolving this confound a higher priority than it would otherwise be.

## 3.9 External validation cohort

The water-policy finding (Section 3.5) is, by construction, a single-cohort observation: the 15-case (11-case, after exclusions) water-policy stratum was part of the same cohort whose composition, substitutions, and analysis choices were fixed before docking results were seen, but the finding itself had never been tested against a second, independently assembled sample. A discovery/validation split was run to check this directly, with every definition -- success threshold, symmetry-corrected RMSD, the paired Wilcoxon test, the 0.3 Å per-case effect threshold -- fixed exactly as specified in Section 2.4 before this second cohort was screened, let alone docked.

A validation cohort was assembled independently of the main cohort's construction: RCSB was queried for single-protein-chain, X-ray structures (resolution ≤2.2 Å) with characterized binding-affinity data, excluding every identifier already used in this study's 60-case cohort or in the 132-identifier registry from the two prior studies by the author's group (Section 1) -- 710 raw candidates. Each was screened locally with the same `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` finding used to define the main cohort's water-policy stratum (Section 2.1), using the largest non-water, non-ion, non-crystallization-additive HETATM component as the candidate ligand; 453 of 710 (64%) qualified. From these, 80 were selected in a fixed, deterministic order (sorted by PDB identifier, not by any docking outcome, since no docking had been run at this stage) with a pre-registered cap of two cases per ligand chemical-component identifier, to avoid a handful of chemical series dominating the sample the way the main cohort capped cases per biological target (Section 2.2). Redocking followed Section 2.4 exactly: both water policies, three seeds, exhaustiveness 32. Sixteen of the 80 failed technical validation on the first pass (Meeko polymer-padding or ligand-protonation failures, the same failure categories as Table S2), each replaced once, in the same fixed order, by the next qualifying, not-yet-used candidate; four of those sixteen replacements also failed, and -- following the same rule already applied to the main cohort (Section 2.2), that only a preparation-stage crash disqualifies a candidate and no second substitution round is run -- were left unsubstituted rather than triggering a further round. The resulting validation cohort has 76 usable cases, within the 60-100 range planned for this check.

The water-policy effect does not replicate. A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the best-of-9 RMSD (conservative vs. simplified, n=76) gives W=1371.0, **p=0.78**, mean paired difference −0.054 Å -- an order of magnitude further from significance than the main cohort's already-non-significant result (n=11, p=0.21), and this cohort is nearly seven times larger. The direction is not even consistent: the simplified policy has a marginally *higher* best-of-9 success fraction (0.803 vs. 0.789) while the conservative policy has a marginally lower mean RMSD, a pattern indistinguishable from noise rather than a weaker version of the same effect. Per-case concentration confirms this: 10 of 76 cases favor retaining water by more than 0.3 Å, 11 favor removing it, and 55 show no effect -- a near-even split, unlike the main cohort's water-policy stratum (5 help, 1 hurt, 5 no-effect at n=11), and this is not simply a smaller version of the same skew diluted by a larger sample: the largest individual helping and hurting cases in this validation cohort (1FCY +3.545 Å, 1N4K −2.504 Å; full per-case data in `benchmark/f7-external-validation/f7_by_case_symmetry.csv`) are comparable in magnitude to the main cohort's largest effects, they simply do not share a common direction across the cohort the way the main cohort's did.

This is reported as a genuine failure to replicate, not reframed as a smaller or more nuanced version of the original finding. A plausible, unconfirmed explanation is chemical-composition mismatch rather than the original finding being a pure statistical fluke: the main cohort's water-policy stratum was found, while diagnosing 1EPP/1PPM (Section 3.4), to be dominated by unusually large, flexible peptidomimetic aspartic-protease inhibitors (mean 14.0 rotatable bonds, more than double the other three strata), whereas this validation cohort was drawn from an unrestricted cross-section of characterized protein-ligand complexes with no comparable enrichment; if the original effect depends on ligand flexibility or a specific active-site chemistry rather than on bridging water generically, a broad validation sample would be expected to wash it out even if the effect is real within its original chemical niche. This study did not test that hypothesis directly -- it would require a validation cohort deliberately matched on ligand flexibility, not just on the bridging-water finding -- and reports it as an open question rather than a rescue of the original claim. The practical conclusion is stated plainly: the water-policy result in Section 3.5 should be read as a single-cohort, exploratory observation that this larger, independent check did not confirm, not as a validated predictor of preparation-policy sensitivity in general use.

## 3.10 Predictive evaluation

A single binary predictor does not admit a swept ROC curve (that requires a continuous score); the single achieved sensitivity/specificity point corresponds to a balanced accuracy of 0.684.

The magnitude relationship is a different, and better-supported, story. Cases where the finding fires show a substantially larger mean |ΔRMSD| than cases where it does not (0.329 Å vs. 0.023 Å, a 14-fold difference; Mann-Whitney U=801.5, p<0.0001) -- a highly significant difference in effect size, even though the binary threshold-crossing classification is weak. This magnitude relationship, unlike the directional water-policy finding itself, is corroborated by the external validation cohort (Section 3.9): despite being 100% positive by construction (every F7 case was screened for this finding, so F7 cannot supply a full confusion matrix, only a check on the positive-predicted class), its mean |ΔRMSD| is 0.372 Å and its median is 0.097 Å -- both closely comparable to the main cohort's flagged-case values (mean 0.329 Å, median 0.110 Å), not to its unflagged-case values (mean 0.023 Å, median 0.000 Å). The finding's PPV also replicates closely (F7: 0.276; main cohort: 0.257).

Read together with Section 3.9, this supports a more precise claim than either "the finding predicts a water-policy benefit" or "the finding predicts nothing": `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` reliably predicts *that* a case's water-policy sensitivity, if any, will tend to be larger in magnitude than an unflagged case's -- a relationship that held up in independent data -- without predicting *which direction* that sensitivity will run, a relationship that did not. A user of this tool should read a positive flag as "test both policies and compare, because if this case is sensitive at all the difference is more likely to matter," not as "retaining water will improve this case's result."

# 4 Discussion

The central, sobering finding of this study is that its own strongest-looking within-cohort result did not survive an independent check. Three preparation-policy manipulations were tested -- a site-local bridging water, alternate-location conformer, or metal ion near the declared ligand, each evaluated with a symmetry-corrected redocking-accuracy metric (Section 2.4) that materially changed two of the three results -- and all three showed a within-cohort pattern consistent with the audit-flagged risk category predicting preparation-policy sensitivity. For water (Section 3.5), the effect was not visible in the binary top-ranked-pose outcome, only in the best-of-nine-poses metric; it was the most consistently one-directional effect of the three, though it did not reach conventional statistical significance in a paired test at this cohort size (p=0.21), and it did not appear in all of the flagged stratum's cases even once cases with no water actually meeting the bridging criterion are excluded (5 of 11 benefit clearly, 1 of 11 is hurt, 5 of 11 show no effect despite a genuine bridging water being present and manipulated). This was the result chosen for external validation precisely because it looked like the strongest candidate to generalize -- and, on a 76-case independent cohort screened for the identical bridging-water finding, its *direction* did not replicate (Section 3.9): p=0.78, no consistent direction, and a per-case split (10 help, 11 hurt, 55 no effect) indistinguishable from noise. A formal predictive evaluation (Section 3.10) found that what did carry over was narrower but real: `SITE_BRIDGING_WATER_PRESENT` predicts *that* a flagged case's water-policy sensitivity will tend to be larger in magnitude than an unflagged case's (mean |ΔRMSD| 0.329 Å vs. 0.023 Å in the main cohort, Mann-Whitney p<0.0001), and this magnitude relationship -- not the direction -- is what the validation cohort actually corroborated (mean/median |ΔRMSD| 0.372/0.097 Å, closely matching the main cohort's flagged-case values, not its unflagged ones). For altLoc (Section 3.6), the effect is a comparable magnitude but split nearly evenly in direction (3 of 4 non-negligible cases favor the default policy, 1 opposes it by a comparable magnitude), rather than the one-directional pattern water showed within its own cohort; a single case's symmetry-correction (Section 2.4) moved this comparison from appearing close to significance (exact-index p=0.064) to clearly not (p=0.21), a genuine change in the conclusion, not a rounding difference, and it was not externally validated at all. For metal (Section 3.7), the effect is the least concentrated of the three (only 2 of 15 cases, 13%, show a >0.3 Å effect) but includes the single largest per-case effect in the entire study (1DTH, −2.756 Å), points in the opposite direction from the project's default assumption -- removing the metal outperforms retaining it, on average and in both non-negligible cases -- and, after the same symmetry correction removed one case whose apparent effect ran against that pattern, is the only one of the three comparisons to reach conventional statistical significance within its own cohort (p=0.0084); it, too, was not externally validated, and given that the study's one externally-tested result failed to replicate, this comparison's untested significance should not be read as more trustworthy than water's turned out to be. Read together, these results support a narrower claim than a purely within-cohort reading would suggest, and a more precise one than a flat "did not replicate" verdict would: an audit flag *can* correlate with preparation-policy sensitivity within a specific, frozen cohort, and this study's own attempt to confirm that on independent data supported the magnitude of that relationship while rejecting its direction, for the one comparison it tested -- which is a reason for skepticism about the direction (though not necessarily the magnitude) of the other two comparisons' within-cohort effects as well, not a reason to treat either finding as confirmed by proxy. The audit flag is offered as a *triage* signal -- a reason to test more than one preparation policy on a given receptor, because a flagged case's policy sensitivity, if present, is likely to be larger than an unflagged case's -- not as a *validated deterministic* rule, not as a predictor of *which direction* a policy change will move the result, not as evidence that any specific unflagged case is necessarily safe under a single policy, and, after Sections 3.9-3.10, not as a claim this study can currently defend beyond that narrower, magnitude-only form.

Why the water effect concentrates in roughly a third of the water-policy stratum rather than all of it is an open question this study does not resolve -- and, given Section 3.9, the more pressing open question is why it concentrates in this specific cohort at all rather than in the broader chemical space the validation cohort sampled, discussed below. A stricter geometric hypothesis -- that the effect should track a hydrogen-bond-range contact between the retained water and a ligand heteroatom -- was tested against the original pilot cohort and rejected (Section 3.5): most cases meeting that criterion showed no measurable effect. A plausible alternative, not tested here, is that the effect depends on whether removing the water leaves a cavity that Vina's search samples poorly, which would be a property of the local binding-site geometry rather than of the water's contact distance alone; testing this would require comparing sampled-pose spatial distributions with and without the water present, not only their best RMSD, and is left for follow-up work. The altLoc effect's near-even direction split (Section 3.6) suggests a different underlying mechanism is at play there: occupancy in the deposited crystal reflects which conformation was more populated experimentally, not which one a rigid-receptor docking search will sample more successfully, so there is no equally strong a priori reason to expect a consistent direction the way there is for a genuine bridging water. The metal effect's dominant case, 1DTH, is itself a sampling-not-scoring story like water's: the top-ranked pose is essentially unchanged between policies, but the best-of-nine pose crosses the success threshold only when the metal is removed (Section 3.7) -- consistent with the rigid metal ion (and, confounded with it, the geometrically forced histidine protonation states) constraining Vina's search away from a near-native pose that becomes accessible once removed. The two cases with the largest reproducible worst-of-both-worlds RMSD in the cohort (1EPP, 1PPM, Section 3.4) illustrate a related point: a case can be a genuinely hard docking problem, independent of any preparation choice tested here, and the audit engine has no mechanism to distinguish "hard for a reason a tested policy addresses" from "hard for an unrelated reason" -- it flags structural features, not docking difficulty. A targeted investigation of why (Section 3.4) found ligand flexibility as a real contributing factor -- and, more broadly, that the water-policy stratum's ligands are on average roughly twice as flexible as the other three strata's (Limitation 10), a previously undocumented imbalance plausibly rooted in bridging water being a well-characterized feature of aspartic-protease active sites specifically -- but not a sufficient explanation on its own, since other similarly flexible cases in the same stratum dock successfully; an unverifiable reference-pose-quality issue, for two structures old enough to predate routine structure-factor deposition, remains an open, unfalsified alternative.

This work is not positioned as a competitor to docking engines or docking-oriented interfaces such as AutoDock Vina itself, Schrödinger's commercial suite, or open-source convenience tools such as AMDock [11]: those tools help a user run docking. DockPrep Audit addresses a different, upstream step -- deciding and documenting how a receptor is prepared before docking is run at all -- and is conceptually closer to structure-validation tools such as MolProbity [12] or protonation tools such as PDB2PQR [13] than to a docking GUI, though neither of those performs the specific risk-stratification or policy-sensitivity role attempted here. A tool at this stage of the pipeline is only useful if its output changes what a researcher does next; the evidence in Sections 3.5-3.7 was offered as a within-cohort demonstration that it can, for all three preparation decisions the engine flags, and Section 3.9's non-replication is offered just as directly, as evidence that a within-cohort demonstration is not sufficient justification on its own. This study varied the water-retention policy uniformly across all four strata (Table 3) and, separately, the altLoc-resolution policy (Table 5) and the metal-retention policy (Table 7), each on the 15 cases where the corresponding audit-flagged feature sits in the binding site -- closing the three-way comparison (altLoc, metal, water) that an earlier version of this manuscript identified as its own limitation -- and then, going a step further than that three-way comparison alone would have required, ran an independent discovery/validation check on the one result best positioned to generalize. That the check failed is reported as the more valuable methodological contribution of the two possible outcomes: a positive replication would have been reassuring, but a pre-registered, transparently reported non-replication is direct evidence that this study's frozen-cohort discipline (Section 2.2) extends to testing its own conclusions, not only its own inputs. The metal result carries its own additional caveat, not shared by water or altLoc: the metal-removal manipulation is confounded with a simultaneous change in coordinating-histidine protonation state (Section 3.7, Limitation 8), so even taken at face value within its own cohort it should be read as evidence that *this specific, compound* preparation choice matters for a minority of cases, not as an isolated test of the metal atom's presence alone -- and it carries the water result's caveat too, since it was never itself checked against independent data.

# 5 Limitations

Twelve limitations are stated explicitly rather than left implicit.

1. **The water model has no explicit desolvation term.** A retained bridging water is represented as a rigid oxygen carrying a fixed TIP3P point charge (Section 2.3); Vina's scoring function was not designed to model the thermodynamic cost or benefit of displacing or retaining an explicit water molecule. The null or near-null result in most water-policy cases should be read with this limitation in mind: it cannot distinguish a genuinely null effect from an effect too weak for this operationalization of "water" to detect. A targeted check on the four largest-effect cases (Section 3.5), re-run with an explicit, hydrogen-bond-oriented TIP3P water in place of the bare oxygen, retained the same effect direction in all four and an equal-or-larger magnitude in three of the four -- evidence against the bare-oxygen simplification being the source of the effect in that subset, though it was not re-run across the full cohort and does not address the desolvation-term gap itself.
2. **Metal ions lack explicit coordination geometry.** Metals are retained with their correct formal charge but without explicit tetrahedral (or other) coordination pseudo-atoms; this is a known limitation of the field's standard tooling, not specific to this pipeline. It does not, however, correspond to a null metal-policy effect: Section 3.7 found a policy-dependent effect in 2 of 15 metal/cofactor cases -- including the largest per-case effect of any manipulation tested in this study (1DTH) -- concentrated enough in one direction to be this study's only formally significant comparison, though that comparison is itself confounded with a coordinating-histidine protonation-state change (Limitation 8).
3. **Overlap of biological target with a prior study by the same author.** Twenty of the 60 frozen cases (33%) share a UniProt-accession biological target with a record already used in the author's own 102-target DUD-E receptor-preparation audit -- six of the original 12 pilot cases, and fourteen of the 48 expansion cases (Table S4). Every overlapping case is nonetheless a distinct PDB entry, and the two studies ask categorically different questions (preparation feasibility at scale vs. preparation-policy sensitivity); replacing these cases after they were already frozen and eligibility-verified was judged a worse trade-off than transparent disclosure, following the same reasoning already applied once in this study to a smaller overlap in the pilot cohort. Requiring fully novel biological targets for a risk-stratified docking benchmark is close to infeasible in practice, since visible altLoc/water/metal richness concentrates in exactly the well-characterized, frequently crystallized targets the field already studies most.
4. **Cohort size relative to the effect being tested.** Fifteen cases per stratum (11 for the water-policy comparison specifically, after excluding the 3 cases with no confirmed bridging water and 1 with no usable symmetry-correction data; Sections 2.3-2.4) is an order of magnitude larger than the 3-per-stratum pilot that first surfaced this question, and was large enough for the water-policy effect to survive as a distributed, multi-case pattern rather than a single-case artifact (Section 3.5). It was not, however, large enough for a formal paired significance test to confirm the water or altLoc effect at conventional thresholds (Sections 3.5-3.6); those two effects are descriptive, not statistically confirmed. The metal comparison did reach significance (Section 3.7) at the same n=15, illustrating that cohort size alone does not determine which comparisons in this study cross that threshold -- effect consistency across cases matters as much as sample size.
5. **Structural-validation coverage does not follow a clean historical cutoff.** Eleven of 60 cases lack RSCC/RSR because no structure-factor file is available at RCSB; this is correlated with, but not a clean function of, pre-1998 deposition (Section 3.2, Fig. 2) -- six cases deposited between 1999 and 2004 also lack this validation, confirmed directly rather than assumed from deposition year alone.
6. **AutoDock Vina's non-determinism.** Even with a fixed seed, Vina is not fully reproducible under multi-threaded execution (`--cpu 0`); this was mitigated, not eliminated, by running three predeclared seeds per case and policy and reporting the observed range alongside the median (Table 3, Table S3).
7. **The water-policy effect is not uniquely threshold-robust.** Recomputing the conservative-vs-simplified success-fraction gap across a range of RMSD success thresholds (0.5-5.0 Å) rather than only the 2.0 Å threshold used throughout this study (Section 3.5, Fig. S1) shows the water-policy gap is positive across most of that range, so it is not an artifact of the single threshold chosen in advance. It is a noisy, non-monotonic curve at n=15 cases per stratum, and the alternate-location stratum shows a comparably sized gap through most of the range and a larger one at 3.2-4.7 Å -- under this same *water*-policy manipulation, not the dedicated altLoc-policy manipulation reported separately in Section 3.6. Readers should not read the threshold-sensitivity check as independent proof that the water-policy effect is uniquely large among the four strata; it confirms only that the effect reported at the pre-specified 2.0 Å threshold is not a cherry-picked artifact of that specific value. This check, and Figs. 3-4, were built before the symmetry-correction refinement (Limitation 9) and use the original exact-index RMSD (Table S3); given how modestly correction moved the water-policy result specifically (Section 3.5), this is not expected to change the qualitative pattern shown, but it was not re-run to confirm that.
8. **The metal-policy manipulation is confounded with coordinating-histidine protonation state.** Removing a metal ion (Section 2.3, Section 3.7) also reverts any histidine whose tautomer was geometrically forced by that metal's coordination back to Meeko's default tautomer, since the geometric rule has no physical basis once the metal is absent. This study cannot separate the metal atom's own contribution from the coordinating-histidine protonation-state change -- the two always move together in the "remove" policy as implemented. This does not appear to be a trivial, universal confound (three other metal-stratum cases have 2-4 similarly affected histidines but show no comparable docking effect), but a dedicated third policy -- metal removed, coordinating-histidine tautomer still geometrically forced as if the metal were present -- would be needed to isolate the two contributions, and was not run here.
9. **Symmetry-corrected RMSD has its own scope limits.** The correction (Section 2.4) enumerates exact graph automorphisms of the ligand as built for docking and takes the minimum-RMSD assignment; it does not correct for near-degenerate cases where a pose is almost, but not exactly, related to another by symmetry (a rotated but not perfectly eclipsed ring, for instance), and by construction it can only ever lower or leave unchanged a reported RMSD relative to the exact-index value, never raise it -- so any comparison between symmetry-corrected and exact-index results should expect the correction to shrink apparent differences on average, not merely move them randomly. One case (1OHR) has no usable automorphism data at all, for a data-source reason unrelated to receptor preparation (Section 2.4), and keeps its exact-index value throughout; this removes it from stratum-level paired comparisons rather than mixing corrected and uncorrected values in the same test.
10. **The water-policy stratum's ligands are systematically more flexible than the other three strata's.** Mean rotatable-bond count is 14.0 (median 15) for the water-policy stratum versus 5.1, 7.9, and 6.2 for alternate location, metal/cofactor, and low-risk control respectively (Section 3.4) -- a roughly two-fold imbalance found while diagnosing the stratum's two most extreme cases (1EPP, 1PPM), not designed into the cohort-selection criteria, and plausibly explained by bridging water being a well-characterized feature of aspartic-protease active sites specifically, a class of large, flexible peptidomimetic-inhibitor complexes. This does not undermine the water-policy comparison itself, which is paired within case (the same ligand's flexibility is identical under both the conservative and simplified policy for that case), but it does mean the water-policy stratum's absolute RMSD values (Table 3) should not be compared at face value against the other three strata's as if all four presented equally hard docking targets -- some fraction of the stratum's higher absolute RMSD is attributable to ligand flexibility rather than to water handling, and this study did not partition how much.
11. **The docking-box formula underestimates the required size for elongated ligands.** $L=2.9\times R_g$ (Eq. 2) is a single-scalar, isotropic-shape formula reproduced verbatim from its source [6] (Section 2.3); checked against this cohort's actual ligand geometries (Section 3.3), it leaves the ligand's own extent poking outside the box in 13 of 60 cases (22%), concentrated among the most elongated ligands rather than the smallest ones. The measured accuracy cost is real but modest (best-of-9 success 0.692 vs. 0.804 for adequately boxed cases) and does not appear to be the primary driver of this study's largest individual failures, which have independently identified causes (Sections 3.4, 3.6). A shape-aware box -- e.g. sized from the ligand's principal-axis extents rather than a single Rg scalar -- would likely recover some of this gap and is a natural refinement for a future revision of the pipeline, not implemented here because it would change every case's box retroactively, after results were already frozen (Section 2.2).
12. **The external validation cohort (Section 3.9) was assembled with less manual curation than the main cohort, which is itself a plausible, uncontrolled contributor to non-replication.** It used a per-ligand-chemical-component cap rather than the main cohort's per-biological-target (UniProt) cap, since a UniProt lookup for several hundred candidates was not run; it restricted candidates to single-protein-chain entries for a simpler receptor-chains determination, rather than the main cohort's individually reviewed, sometimes-overridden `REMARK 350`-based chain assignment (Section 2.2); and no wwPDB RSCC/RSR or biological-assembly check (Section 3.2) was run on it at all. None of these were tightened or loosened after seeing the non-replication result -- they were the protocol before any of the 76 cases were docked -- but they mean the validation cohort is not a strict apples-to-apples repeat of the main cohort's construction, only an independent sample screened for the same site-local finding. This is offered as one more candidate explanation for Section 3.9's result, alongside the ligand-flexibility hypothesis discussed there, not as a reason to discount the non-replication.

# 6 Conclusions

This study tested three preparation-policy manipulations -- retaining vs. removing site-local bridging water, applied uniformly across a 60-case, four-stratum benchmark; highest- vs. lowest-occupancy altLoc resolution; and retaining vs. removing the metal ion, the latter two each applied to the 15 cases with the corresponding site-local audit-flagged feature -- and, using a symmetry-corrected redocking-accuracy metric throughout (Section 2.4), asked whether the audit-flagged risk category predicts where each manipulation's effect concentrates. Within its own frozen cohort, it does, conditionally, for all three, though not uniformly: retaining bridging water produces a reproducible, multi-case improvement in best-of-nine-poses accuracy in the water-policy stratum, invisible under a binary top-ranked-pose threshold and not reaching formal statistical significance at this cohort size (n=11 confirmed cases, p=0.21); the altLoc-resolution choice produces a comparable-magnitude effect in the alternate-location stratum, split almost evenly between the two possible directions rather than favoring one policy consistently, and -- once a symmetry-labeling artifact in one case is corrected -- does not approach significance either (p=0.21); the metal-retention choice produces the least concentrated effect of the three (2 of 15 cases) yet includes the single largest per-case effect in the entire study, points in the direction opposite the project's default assumption -- removing the metal outperforming retaining it -- and is the only one of the three manipulations to reach conventional statistical significance within its own cohort (p=0.0084), though that comparison is itself confounded with a coordinating-histidine protonation-state change and cannot be attributed to the metal atom alone (Limitation 8). Critically, this within-cohort pattern did not fully hold up under the one external check this study actually ran: the water-policy result's *direction*, tested against an independently assembled, 76-case validation cohort screened for the identical bridging-water finding, did not replicate (p=0.78, no consistent direction). A formal predictive evaluation (Section 3.10) found a narrower claim that did hold: the audit finding predicts the *magnitude* of policy sensitivity a case is likely to show (a 14-fold difference in mean |ΔRMSD| between flagged and unflagged cases, Mann-Whitney p<0.0001), and this magnitude relationship, specifically, is what the external cohort corroborated, even though its direction did not. The practical implication is stated narrowly, and more cautiously than the within-cohort numbers alone would suggest: an audit-detected bridging water, alternate conformer, or metal ion near the ligand is a reason to test more than one preparation policy on that specific receptor because, if it is sensitive to the choice at all, that sensitivity is likely to be larger than in an unflagged case -- not a validated predictor of *which* policy will perform better, since this study's own attempt to confirm that narrower, directional claim on independent data failed to do so; the altLoc and metal results were never put through the same check and their within-cohort significance (or lack of it) should be read with that specifically in mind. The metal result additionally illustrates that a symmetry-uncorrected RMSD is not a neutral simplification, since it changed which of the three comparisons this study would have reported as its strongest finding, before the external check reset which of them deserved that description at all. DockPrep Audit, the versioned cohort (including the 76-case validation cohort), and the analysis pipeline are released to allow this test to be repeated, extended to a metal-policy comparison that isolates the coordinating-histidine confound, or -- given what Section 3.9 found -- applied to further independent cohorts before any of these three results are relied upon.
